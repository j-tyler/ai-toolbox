# Change Intent

Change Intent is a lightweight, cooperative workflow contract carried by a durable per-change artifact. The artifact helps the author make the intended change deliberate, gives implementation a goal and decision boundary, gives review a consistent target, and preserves the change's purpose and boundaries for future agents. The contract establishes a small common shape: direction is made explicit before implementation, implementation works from that direction, and review assesses the resulting change against it. Beyond that common shape, the design aims to fit within a team's existing structure and practices. Teams retain substantial latitude over who or what occupies each role, how those roles integrate with existing development practices, where evidence and findings live, and how review results affect merge decisions.

Change intent serves five purposes. They fit together, and each one shapes the design:

1. **The author gets clarity.** Stating what a change must make true — before code exists to anchor on — surfaces the fuzzy spots in the author's own thinking. This got harder with AI, not easier: direction that used to be sharpened by the labor of implementation now needs its own deliberate moment.

2. **AI gets a platform to drive against.** An approved intent is specific about what the change is and what must hold for it to be done — exactly the shape a coding harness's goal mechanism needs. The implementation agent works autonomously toward completion instead of being re-prompted turn by turn.

3. **The human reviewer gets consistency.** With AI adoption uneven across a team, changes arrive developed in ways that vary by teammate, tool, and day. When every change passes through the same authored, implemented, reviewed frame, the reviewer can trust the shape of the process and spend their judgment on the change itself.

4. **Future agents get the context.** The reasoning behind a change used to die with the pull request, because no future human would dig it up. Future AI agents will — they read history cheaply and act on what they find — so the highest-value tokens of the pre-code dialogue are worth saving into the repository.

5. **The design points forward.** Every seat is a logical responsibility, not a job title, so the same artifact and contract keep working as humans author less and review less.

These purposes are also the bar for changing this design: a modification is accepted only when it serves at least one of them **and** adds no required step, wait, or artifact for the team — the used-beats-better tradeoff from [design.md](design.md), applied as a test.

The most immediate payoff is **reviewability**: less focus on the change itself, more focus on the review process around it. By the time a change reaches human review, it carries a visible chain of custody: an approved intent, the resulting implementation and tests, any implementation-time amendments, completion against the goal, and an independent review assessment. Here chain of custody means cooperative continuity through the team's existing workflow, not an audit trail or mechanically verified provenance. At the core workflow level, the intent file is the additional durable per-change artifact; teams may preserve further implementation or review evidence when it benefits their environment. This gives the reviewer a consistent frame for how AI developed and assessed the change and where human judgment is most valuable. Today that means reviewing better; over time, it means reviewing less. This is an idea related to [Working in Public](../working-in-public/README.md) — the principle of capturing the high-value outputs of AI work in artifacts that persist beyond the session that produced them, so future agents can reference them; change intent does this for the pre-code dialogue that figures out what a change should be.

A change intent is a structured artifact whose initial form is approved *before implementation begins*. It is **complete over change-defining decisions and open over implementation**: it records the outcomes, guarantees, constraints, and exclusions that distinguish the change without attempting to prescribe every technical choice or incidental effect. A fork is change-defining only when choosing between its branches decides which change will be delivered; if the author could approve once and allow either branch while still receiving the approved change, the fork is implementation latitude. Completeness is the standard for the artifact and the authoring process, not a claim that an agent can prove the absence of unknown dependencies. The implementation agent may record a narrow amendment if a claim cannot be delivered within the approved boundaries — the current intent's outcomes, constraints, and exclusions, together with applicable project instructions — or implementation reveals a necessary change-defining decision that the approved intent does not settle. If returned work changes the author's direction before merge, the author may replace the current unmerged intent; the replacement is approved and implementation and review run again against it. Only the intent that merges becomes the persistent record.

## Adoption fit

Change intent is intended for teams that delegate material implementation or review work to AI agents and want the decisions behind a change to remain independent of the code that happens to be produced. Adoption requires the team to approve an initial intent before implementation begins, reassess retained work whenever the author replaces an unmerged intent, and preserve merged intents as historical records. It is not a process for exploratory work, a substitute for ordinary engineering review, or a template to be backfilled after implementation.

Adoption uses author, implementation, and review as logical seats in the workflow, not necessarily three distinct people, agents, services, or organizational stages. A team may assign or combine those seats within its existing practices as long as each responsibility is fulfilled. The author decides the change-defining questions surfaced before approval. The implementation agent may choose unconstrained technical details without further approval and may use the amendment process when implementation proves that an approved claim cannot be delivered within the approved boundaries or exposes a missing necessary decision. A missing-decision amendment is a provisional implementation-time ruling that the author reviews when the work returns. The reviewer independently assesses the implementation and its evidence, using the capabilities available in the team's review operation and inference to determine whether each acceptance criterion has a proving test and whether each invariant holds across the affected change. `Cannot verify` is a valid review result that identifies the limit of the available evidence, not a failure of the review process. These role boundaries allow work to continue without turning the intent into an exhaustive specification.

**Outcomes** and **Why** are always included: what the change is intended to make true, and the problem, event, or need that caused the change to be made. Why explains the change; it does not establish requirements or implementation direction.

The change intent file also includes any of the following when their conditions apply:

- **Constraints** — conditions and non-behavioral boundaries that every acceptable implementation must be designed around. They guide implementation and review but are not automatically proof obligations; lack of proof is not a violation.
- **Acceptance criteria** — falsifiable scenarios that prove selected outcomes, guarantees, or guardrails, each closed by a focused proving test. "When a user does X, the system returns Y." They are proof obligations for the decisions the change makes, not an inventory of everything implementation may make observable.
- **Invariants** — properties that must hold *across* the parts of the system affected by the change. Tests should exercise them where useful, but no passing test closes an invariant by itself; implementation and review must also reason across the affected diff and relevant surrounding paths. The intent states the property, not an inventory of locations or tests. "Read-after-write holds across all caller paths of `GetPlaylist`."
- **Out of scope** — what this change explicitly is *not* doing. Signals conscious exclusions to the implementation agent, the AI review pass, and the human reviewer. Applies when there are conscious exclusions worth signaling.
- **Amendments** — repairs made during implementation, when a claim could not be delivered within the approved boundaries or a necessary change-defining decision surfaced after approval. Each entry records the discovered fact and quotes the affected item's previous and current wording. The current intent remains understandable without reading this history. The author rules on any amendment when the work returns.

Each section serves a distinct downstream purpose: Why carries motivation to future readers; acceptance criteria are mechanically checked test-by-test; invariants invite property thinking across the diff; constraints bound engineering judgment without demanding proof that the available environment cannot provide; out of scope signals conscious exclusion to every downstream consumer; amendments record how the approved intent changed when implementation exposed an error or missing decision. An omitted section indicates that its condition does not apply.
